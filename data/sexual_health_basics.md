Q: What is an STI, and how common are they?
A: An STI (sexually transmitted infection) is an infection passed between people primarily through sexual contact. Common examples include chlamydia, gonorrhea, HPV, herpes, and HIV. They are very common — many people will encounter one at some point — and having one is not a reflection of someone's character or worth. Some STIs show no symptoms at all, which is why regular testing matters even if you feel fine.
Source: https://www.cdc.gov/sti/about/index.html

---

Q: How can someone protect themselves from STIs?
A: The most effective methods are using barrier protection like condoms consistently and correctly, getting vaccinated where vaccines exist (such as for HPV), and getting tested regularly, especially with a new partner. Condoms, when used consistently and correctly, are highly effective — for example, about 98% effective against HIV transmission specifically.
Source: https://www.cdc.gov/std/prevention

Q: Where can someone actually go to get tested or ask questions confidentially?
A: Options include a family doctor or gynecologist, a local Planned Parenthood or family planning clinic, or a walk-in/urgent care clinic. Many of these offer confidential services, meaning your information isn't automatically shared with parents or others, though confidentiality rules can vary by country and age — it's okay to directly ask the clinic about their confidentiality policy before sharing details.
Source: https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn

Q: What does "consent" actually mean?
A: Consent means clearly and freely agreeing to take part in something — it must be given willingly, without pressure, manipulation, or fear, and can be taken back at any time, even partway through. A person cannot meaningfully give consent if they are pressured, asleep, underage relative to the other person, or under the influence of drugs or alcohol.
Source: https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn/sex-and-relationships

Q: What's the difference between normal nervousness and a relationship or situation that isn't okay?
A: Nervousness or shyness about a new experience is normal. What is NOT okay is anyone pressuring you, making you feel guilty for saying no, threatening to end a relationship if you don't comply, or ignoring you when you say stop. Healthy relationships involve open communication and respect for boundaries — feeling consistently pressured or afraid is a sign to talk to a trusted adult.
Source: https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn/sex-and-relationships

Q: Can someone get pregnant even if their periods are irregular?
A: Yes. Irregular periods do not mean someone can't get pregnant — ovulation can still happen even when the cycle isn't predictable. This is a common misconception, especially among teens whose cycles are naturally still settling into a pattern.
Source: https://myhealth.ucsd.edu/3,89561

Q: What is emergency contraception, and when is it used?
A: Emergency contraception (sometimes called the "morning-after pill") is used after unprotected sex or contraception failure to reduce the chance of pregnancy. It is most effective the sooner it's taken after the event, and is generally available at pharmacies without needing a prescription, though rules vary by country.
Source: https://www.womenshealth.gov/a-z-topics/emergency-contraception

Q: Is it normal to feel confused, curious, or awkward about sexual health topics?
A: Completely normal. Curiosity about your body and relationships is a healthy part of growing up. What's harmful isn't the questions themselves — it's not having a safe, accurate place to ask them, which pushes people toward guesswork, myths, or unreliable sources instead of real answers.
Source: https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn

---

Q: If I think I might have an STI, what should I actually do?
A: See a healthcare provider or visit a clinic like Planned Parenthood as soon as possible for testing — most STIs are very treatable, especially when caught early, and clinics handle this routinely without judgment. Avoiding testing out of fear or shame is the biggest risk factor for complications, not the infection itself.
Source: https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn/stds-hiv-safer-sex

---

Q: Does using contraception (like the pill) protect against STIs too?
A: No — hormonal contraception (like the pill, IUD, or implant) prevents pregnancy but does not protect against STIs. Only barrier methods like condoms protect against both. Using condoms alongside another contraception method (called dual protection) gives the strongest combined protection.
Source: https://www.cdc.gov/std/prevention

---

Q: Who can someone talk to if they don't feel comfortable asking a parent about sexual health?
A: A school nurse or counselor, a trusted doctor, or confidential clinics like Planned Parenthood are all designed to answer these questions without judgment, and many offer services independent of parental involvement, depending on local laws. Not having a comfortable home conversation doesn't mean there's nowhere safe to ask.
Source: https://www.plannedparenthood.org/learn
